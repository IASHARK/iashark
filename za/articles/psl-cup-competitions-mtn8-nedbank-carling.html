MTN8, Nedbank Cup, Carling Knockout:
the PSL cups explained

South African football has more than one trophy on offer every season. Here is how the three main cup competitions work, and why a knockout tie should never be read like a league fixture.

1. More than one trophy a season

In South African football, the league title is only one of several prizes available to top-flight clubs. Alongside the Betway Premiership, three cup competitions shape the season: the MTN8, the Nedbank Cup and the Carling Knockout. Each has its own entry rules and format, and each changes how clubs manage their squads.

For anyone reading fixtures with data, cups matter twice. They add matches to an already busy calendar, and a knockout tie follows a different logic from a league match. This guide explains how the three competitions work, then what that means for analysis.

2. The MTN8: a reward for last season

The MTN8 is, as its name suggests, a competition for eight clubs. Entry is earned rather than drawn: the clubs that finished in the top eight positions of the previous season's league take part. Finishing in the top half of the log therefore carries a reward that extends into the following campaign.

The format mixes single matches and two-legged ties. Under the competition rules, the first round and the final are played as single matches, while both semi-finals are played over two legs, home and away, with the club scoring the higher aggregate number of goals going through to the final. The competition takes place in the early part of the season, which means it overlaps with the opening rounds of the league.

That timing matters. Early in a season, league form is still unreliable, squads may still be changing, and a club pushing for an early trophy may manage its players differently in the surrounding league fixtures.

3. The Nedbank Cup: the open knockout

The Nedbank Cup is South Africa's main knockout cup, and its identity rests on one idea: giving lower-league and amateur teams the chance to face the country's top clubs. According to the competition's own explanation of its format, the first round proper features 32 clubs. Half of them are the 16 Betway Premiership sides, who enter automatically. Eight more come from the second-tier Motsepe Foundation Championship, and the final eight come from leagues run by the South African Football Association, either the ABC Motsepe League or the SAB Regional League.

The prize goes beyond the trophy. Under the current allocation, the Nedbank Cup winners earn a place in the CAF Confederation Cup, which gives the competition a continental dimension and a real reason for top clubs to take it seriously.

Ties between a Premiership club and a team from several levels below are exactly where cup football gets its reputation for upsets. They are also where data is weakest: detailed statistics on third- and fourth-tier sides are scarce, so any estimate of such a match carries very wide uncertainty.

4. The Carling Knockout: the league cup returns

The Carling Knockout Cup was introduced in 2023, bringing back a league cup after the Telkom Knockout was discontinued in 2020. It follows the same principle as its predecessor: the 16 Premiership clubs are drawn into a straight knockout competition until a winner is found.

Because only top-flight clubs take part, the Carling Knockout avoids the extreme mismatches of the Nedbank Cup. Every tie is between Premiership sides, which makes league data more relevant, although the knockout format still changes how teams approach each match.

5. Why a cup tie is not a league match

Reading a cup fixture as if it were an ordinary league game is one of the most common analytical mistakes. Several differences matter:

  • Drawing is not a neutral outcome. In a league, a draw is worth a point. In a knockout match, it leads to whatever the rules of that round require next, such as extra time or penalties, so the balance of risk changes late in the game.
  • Two legs change the first leg. In a two-legged semi-final, the first match is only half of the tie. A team can be content with a narrow result away from home, knowing the return leg is still to come.
  • Selection changes. Clubs juggling league, cups and CAF football rotate. A cup line-up can look very different from a league one, which weakens any comparison based on the usual first eleven.
  • Motivation differs by competition. A trophy that leads to a continental place, as the Nedbank Cup does, may be prioritised differently from one that does not. That is hard to measure, so treat it as context rather than a conclusion.
  • Lower-league data is thin. When one side comes from outside the Premiership, the usual statistical tools have far less to work with.

6. "Result" and "to qualify" are different questions

This distinction deserves its own section, because it causes real confusion. A match result market is usually settled on the score after 90 minutes plus stoppage time. A market on which team goes through takes the whole tie into account, including extra time and penalties where they apply. The same match can therefore produce a draw in one sense and a winner in the other. Rules vary between operators and competitions, so always check how a market is settled before drawing any conclusion from its price.

To compare a price with an estimate, convert it into an implied probability first: with decimal odds, divide 1 by the price. At 2.50, the implied probability is 40%. Then remember that a small gap between the market's view and a model's view sits within the margin of error.
